Can a browser measure your reaction time?

Try a short experiment. Work out a median. Then ask the more interesting question: what else is part of that number?

THE QUESTION

A measurement is also a design.

Reaction-time tasks appear to report a single personal number. In a browser, the displayed result also depends on the display, input path, timing conditions and how the task handles missing or unusually late responses. This activity makes those influences visible without ranking students.

THE ACTIVITY

One class. Four steps.

Suggested for secondary-level science, computing or media-literacy lessons. Adapt the timing and participation options to your group.

  1. 013 minutes

    Predict before you measure

    Ask: “If the same person uses two different computers, must they get the same result?” Write down two things that might change the number.

  2. 027 minutes

    Explore one short browser task

    Open Riflessi's short signal task. Read its preparation and consent information before starting. Students who prefer not to participate can work from the hypothetical worksheet values below. Do not collect or display personal results.

  3. 038 minutes

    Find the middle, not the winner

    Sort the five fictional observations in the worksheet and find their median. Compare it with the average. Ask what happens when one observation is unusually long—and why a median alone cannot describe the entire session.

  4. 047 minutes

    Design a fairer repeat

    Discuss display refresh, keyboard versus touch input, interruptions and task instructions. Write a plan that keeps the setup similar. A different device result does not, by itself, identify which influence caused the difference.

TEACHER NOTES

Teach the uncertainty.

The arithmetic

The example's sorted values are 195, 213, 220, 226 and 390 ms. The median is 220 ms and the mean is 248.8 ms. Both summarize those five invented observations; neither establishes a person's underlying ability.

The model

Present display and input delays as influences, not as calibrated components you can simply subtract. Browsers and consumer devices do not independently measure each element of the end-to-end path.

Participation and accessibility

Offer the same learning objectives through the fictional readings to anyone who cannot or does not want to take the timed task. Avoid public scores, ability comparisons, prizes and diagnostic labels. The activity requires no sound or colour discrimination.

Interpretation

A number from one run describes that task under those conditions. Display/input delays and attention can vary. Repeating with a similar setup makes a comparison more interpretable, but it does not validate the measurement as clinical or occupational data.
